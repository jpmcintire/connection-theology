# The Connected Self in Every State

## The woodcarver, the cello beginner, and being held in God

**Connection Theology · 18 November 2026 · vA**

**Origin and status.** The central argument and its decisive return to the beginner are John's, developed in this conversation. This paper records that clarification affirmatively within CT's account of the constituted self. The organization, examples, and explanatory formulations are the assistant's development. It is a new companion to *The Connected Self*, *Agency, Wisdom, and the Return of the Self*, and *Learning to Feel Held*. It does not silently rewrite those papers or turn the conversation into a new proof of universal determinism. The selected original statements are preserved in Appendix A; the relationship to earlier sources and the status of the further inferences are stated in §12.

## 1. The conclusion and the route to it

The master woodcarver is acting through what she has become. Her perception, understanding, bodily skill, attention, and responsiveness can guide her work without a separate conscious instruction for each cut. We can recognize this activity as deeply hers precisely in its unforced expression of her developed capacities.

Now give the same woman a cello she is beginning to learn. She hesitates, recalls instructions, considers finger placement, listens, chooses, and corrects herself. She experiences decisions everywhere. Yet none of this introduces a second kind of author. These activities also occur through what she presently is. Her deliberate beginnerhood is no less an activity of her actual self than her accomplished carving.

That return is the central argument. The first example loosens the identification of self with a conscious director. The second prevents us from relocating the real self into the less visible or less deliberate parts of ourselves. Conscious choosing comes fully back into the account.

There are real differences between mastery and beginnerhood: skill, ease, awareness, adequacy, enjoyment, and the feeling of being oneself. Those differences do not divide the occasions on which the constituted self is present from occasions on which something else takes its place. They describe different activities and experiences of the person.

Across those differences, mind is contextualizing through a particular constitution. The body helps constitute both what is at stake and the perspective from which it is encountered. The mind can contextualize those conditions, and that understanding can itself participate in the next moment of the person's formation. No final examiner outside this process is required.

In CT's theological language, this changing particular life is already held in God: the context with no outside. Being held does not begin when the person masters a craft, stops deliberating, understands the connected self, or feels connected. The recognition is an occurrence within the relation it recognizes.

The result therefore reaches ordinary conscious choice. We do not need to wait for the experience of directing ourselves to recede before we can understand ourselves as connected.

## 2. Why determination can seem to threaten the self

The threatening picture begins by locating the real person in the part of experience that seems to issue decisions: I consider the alternatives, select one, and direct what happens. If that director is taken to be the self, then an account of the conditions producing its thoughts can seem to leave progressively less of the person available as an author.

The fear has a particular structure. If a thought came through capacities, needs, relationships, or processes I did not consciously choose, perhaps it did not really come from me. If the choosing itself arose through those conditions, perhaps the person who seemed to choose has been explained away.

CT's account of self challenges the separation on which that fear depends. A self is a local context with an outside, constituted by its present capacities, dependencies, relationships, and activity. There is no further person behind those constituents who must first receive them and then decide whether to use them. The organized capacities through which the person considers and responds belong to the person. [S1, §§0–4; S8, §10.1]

Present constitution matters here. A past encounter can leave a present capacity, memory, disposition, commitment, or continuing relationship. That does not make the whole past event literally present in every respect. What is operative now belongs in the account of the current person. The person is also open to new encounters and further formation. [S8, §10.6]

The conceptual reply is therefore available: explaining how an activity occurs through the person need not eliminate the person doing it. But that reply can remain difficult to inhabit. Someone may understand the proposition and still feel that the important part of themselves has been taken away.

The woodcarver supplies an experiential route into the distinction. It begins with a case in which the absence of stepwise self-direction need not feel like an absence of self at all.

## 3. The woodcarver and the return to the beginner

### 3.1 The first reversal

Consider the accomplished carver at work. The grain, the resistance of the wood, the angle of the tool, the pressure of the hand, and the emerging form bear on one another in her activity. She can respond through relationships she does not have to recite.

The example does not require her to be unconscious. She may be intensely aware of the wood and of what she is making. What recedes is the need to observe herself issuing an instruction before each movement. Awareness of the activity and explicit supervision of its production are distinguishable.

In the experience John is invoking, this is readily recognized as deep self-expression. Her acquired capacities find expression with little apparent distance between understanding and doing. We accept that the work comes through much that she is, including much she cannot itemize in the moment.

This gives the first reversal its force. If a separate conscious act of originating each step were necessary for an activity to be fully hers, then this familiar way of recognizing mastery would be difficult to explain. Yet the lack of such an act can be part of what makes the work feel so thoroughly hers.

At this stage, it is tempting to conclude that conscious deliberation is a shallower self and absorbed activity is the deeper, more authentic one. Some of the conversation's earlier formulations leaned in that direction. They helped loosen the initial identification, but they did not finish the argument.

### 3.2 The same woman takes up the cello

John's decisive move was to keep the person the same and change the activity.

The master carver is now a beginner at the cello. Her bodily capacities and developed understanding do not yet provide the same fluency in this task. She remembers a rule, tries a placement, hears a result, considers an alternative, and tries again. Her experience contains many explicit decisions.

Her uncertainty is real. So is the effort of deliberation. But what is doing this work?

It is the person who can hear, attend, remember, compare, anticipate, care about the result, and respond. These capacities may be uneven or poorly coordinated for the new activity. They are nevertheless capacities through which she is acting. The thought “That note was wrong; should I move my finger?” is something this person is doing.

A separate chooser does not arrive when the question becomes explicit. Nor must the decision pass through an additional self after the comparing and considering have occurred. Those activities already belong to her present constitution.

Her beginning cello playing is therefore no less from her actual self than her carving. It is less accomplished cello playing. It may feel less comfortable, less expressive, or less like the person she knows herself to be. Those are differences worth retaining. They are not evidence that the self has become less actual.

### 3.3 The return completes the correction

The master first helps us recognize constituted activity as fully ours. The beginner then shows that conscious deliberation belongs under that same recognition.

This is more than adding unconscious processes to a previously conscious self. It removes the need to divide authorship into two kinds: one supposedly independent when consciously witnessed, the other merely caused when it happens without explicit supervision.

The person is present in both. Conscious choosing is among the activities of the connected self.

Nor does the conclusion install an invisible true self beneath the visible one. The operative skill of the master and the tentative reasoning of the beginner are both constituted activities. There is no privileged hidden region into which the real person must be relocated.

This distinction also gives learning its full place. The beginner's listening, questioning, correction, and practice can participate in forming capacities that later operate more fluently. She need not step outside her formation to participate in its continuation. Learning is one of the ways this constituted person changes.

The cello example thus corrects the hierarchy the first reversal could accidentally create. Deliberation does not have to disappear for the activity to be hers. Mastery makes one form of self-expression available; beginnerhood remains a complete occasion of actual selfhood.

### 3.4 What the example establishes

The example is a way of making CT's account intelligible through contrasting experiences. Its central inference uses the already stated account of self as constituted activity. It is not an empirical claim that every human reports mastery or beginnerhood in the same way.

The structure is sufficient for its purpose: where we accept the master's organized activity as hers, the appearance of explicit reasoning in the same person supplies no reason to introduce an independent author. Different experiences of agency can occur within the same account of constituted agency.

“I am choosing” can be a true description of the person considering and acting. The further claim that this occurrence requires a chooser independent of the person's constitution is a different claim. The first need not carry the second. [S5, §§5.3–5.5; S8, §§10.1–10.3]

## 4. Several dimensions of experience and one account of self

John described a continuum involving low and high occlusion, felt self, and intellectual acknowledgment of self. These should be retained as distinguishable dimensions. The argument does not require that they be measurable on a single scale or that their variations be statistically independent.

| Dimension | What can vary | What that variation does not establish by itself |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skill and familiarity | A person can be accomplished in one activity and a beginner in another. | How much actual self is present. |
| Explicit deliberation | An action can involve extensive conscious comparison or very little stepwise instruction. | Whether a separate, unconditioned chooser is operating. |
| Occlusion of formation | More or less of the conditions shaping an activity can be available to understanding. | Whether the unavailable conditions belong to the person's constitution. |
| Felt self | An activity can feel deeply expressive, unfamiliar, divided, or ordinary. | The existence or absence of the constituted person doing it. |
| Intellectual recognition | The person can understand connected selfhood clearly, dimly, or not at all. | Whether their actual connections exist. |

One person can feel most herself in an activity she scarcely examines. The same person can closely examine an activity in which she feels awkward. She can intellectually recognize both as hers. The variations do not have to line up.

Occlusion needs particular care. Several different things can be absent from awareness: the history and present conditions of a response, an explicit narration of how it is being produced, or surrounding matters excluded by absorption. Those absences are not interchangeable.

A beginner's visible deliberation does not make all the conditions of that deliberation visible. A master can understand much about her formation while leaving the performance of a skill largely unnarrated. It would therefore be a mistake to assign all masters “high occlusion” and all beginners “low occlusion” merely from their degree of explicit thought.

The positive conclusion is precise: becoming aware of more of the activity does not move the person outside their constitution, and becoming aware of less does not remove the activity from the person.

Feeling more oneself can remain a meaningful description. It may concern integration, ease, recognition, expression, or the significance of an activity. It need not mean that a larger quantity of literal self has appeared. The differences in lived experience can be substantial without becoming a hierarchy of actual existence.

This is also the sense in which the conversation's “same essence” should be read. It names the continuing applicability of connected, constituted selfhood across these variations. It does not add an invariant inner substance behind the changing constituents. Nor does it erase the real differences between the states.

## 5. Mind first includes the mind at work in skill

The conversation put mind first because it was asking about representation, stakes, and the significance of how a life goes. In CT's broad account, the relevant bearer holds something as of something, with its next step guided through that account. A stake is an actual dependency of such a bearer's next step on something. The dependency need not be recognized by another observer, or explicitly noticed by its holder at that moment, to be real. [S4, §5]

This explanatory priority does not make conscious commentary the core against which other activity is secondary. Thinking, in CT's sense, includes holding, distinguishing, anticipating, and responding to something as something. An acquired skill can embody relationships that guide activity without being restated in words. A particular account can also fail to fit while remaining an account. Mind is not restricted to successful explicit reasoning. [S2, “The claim and the posture” and “The examples that tested the account”; S3, §6]

The carver is therefore contextualizing. The angle of a cut matters in relation to the grain, the tool, the pressure, and the intended form. Her understanding can be operative in the activity. Its correspondence concerns how adequately those relationships are read and used.

The intended form does not have to exist already. It can be held as a possibility to be made. The relevant questions include what the material permits, what the next cut would do, and how that bears on what she is trying to produce. Imagining a form and reporting that it is already present are different uses of representation.

The cello beginner is contextualizing too. A rule is being related to a hand position, a sound, a task, and an attempt. Her work may need more explicit comparison because the relationships are not yet available in the same practiced organization. This remains the activity of mind.

Thus “mind first” and the woodcarver's relatively unnarrated activity do not conflict. The conflict arose only if mind was identified with the visible stream of instructions. CT's account already includes both the operative skill and the deliberate attempt.

Considering a mind apart from familiar human embodiment can help identify this structure. It does not establish the constitution or experience of any actual artificial system. A hypothetical nonhuman mind would still have a position, dependencies, and some organization through which its activity occurs. Its continuity, memory, relationships, and temporal experience would need their own account. Nothing in the carver example settles those questions by analogy.

## 6. The body changes both the case and its appearance

The human doing this contextualizing is an embodied person. The body is among the person's present constituents; it helps make possible the activity through which anything is understood.

Bodily constitution has distinguishable roles. It can change what actually happens to the person and what is at stake. It can also shape attention, felt importance, attraction, aversion, urgency, and the manner in which the situation is encountered. The two roles may coincide, but neither can be substituted for the other.

The conversation's point is not that every bodily event has the function of producing a better account of reality. A need or a felt aversion need not begin as the conclusion of an explicit inquiry. It can nevertheless become part of the situation the mind understands. Where a feeling already presents something as threatening, desirable, or burdensome, it may itself have a contextualizing role. These are distinctions of function within one person, not a division into two independent beings.

Consider a person who is exhausted when an ordinary request arrives. The request feels intolerable, and the person thinks, “You are asking too much of me.”

There is an actual condition of depleted capacity. There is an aversive experience. There is a request from another person. And there is a judgment about that request. The exhaustion may make engagement genuinely costly. The aversion is an actual experience. Neither, by itself, settles whether the request is unreasonable.

Understanding this does not require deleting the person's own burden. The burden belongs in the account alongside what the other person needs. A response might involve explaining a limit, changing the timing, asking for help, or accepting a cost for a sufficient reason. The particular conclusion depends on the relationships in the case.

The important distinction is between what constitutes a stake, what discloses or obscures it, what helps produce a response, and what warrants that response. A single feeling can play more than one of these roles. Its reality does not make every associated interpretation accurate, and an inaccurate interpretation does not make the experience unreal. [S5, §9; S6, §§3–4 and 14]

Embodiment therefore supplies much of the substance to which contextualization must answer. Human needs and enjoyment have their own place in the account. Their significance does not depend entirely on usefulness to somebody else. Equally, their being one's own does not establish every priority claimed for them.

There is no purified mind outside the person that must judge whether to admit the body. The person doing the inquiry is the embodied conjunction under examination.

## 7. The experience and its interpretation enter what happens next

The recursive structure becomes clearer when the person's account of themselves is included.

First, the person's constitution shapes an encounter. Some of those conditions are available to awareness and some are not. Second, the person experiences the encounter and may interpret it. Third, that interpretation can participate in the next response. What happens next then becomes another part of the situation to understand.

Return to the exhausted person. “My exhaustion is contributing to how this request appears to me” can be a new understanding of the same encounter. If that recognition changes the person's response, the recognition is among the conditions through which the next event occurs. It has not reached the case from outside.

The same structure applies to the experience of choosing. John proposed that occlusion can help explain why a determined person experiences themselves as independently free. Much of the formation of a thought is unavailable, while the experience of considering and choosing is vividly present. The person may take that experience as evidence of an independent origin.

The occurrence and its interpretation need to be distinguished. The experienced deliberation is real. The account “this must originate in a chooser beyond my constitution” can be mistaken. If retained or acted upon, that account is itself a real part of what shapes the person.

The resulting picture is not a mixture of real things and literally unreal things. A mistaken representation is a real occurrence with content that fails to fit what it claims. Its effects can be real as well. The same distinction applies when the person revises the account and comes to understand themselves as constituted. [S3, §4a]

The general psychological claim that occlusion is the sole cause of every experience of freedom would require further evidence. The present argument needs less: whatever is unavailable to awareness remains part of the account of the activity, and what the person experiences and thinks about that activity also belongs in the account.

A determined mind can therefore be genuinely changed through representing. The content of an encounter depends on what reaches it as well as on its present capacities. Where the resulting representation persists and remains operative, it becomes part of the subsequent constitution. The mind can become something beyond what it previously was, and beyond what it could have supplied considered in isolation, while remaining wholly within connected activity.

The crucial sentence is: the mind's account of its own formation can itself participate in that formation.

This recursion has no final observer standing above it. A later understanding is another act of the same situated participant. It can be adequate at the question it answers without becoming a complete view of every condition of its own occurrence.

Nor does the account require an endless examination of each examination. The time, effort, and consequences of further inquiry are among the actual dependencies to consider. There can be sufficient reason to act, rest, or stop. [S3, §§4a and 14a]

## 8. Determination and actual participation

The conversation explored its central examples under a deterministic premise. Under that premise, both the master's skill and the beginner's deliberation are determined. The appearance of explicit choice does not create an exception for the latter.

That does not make the comparing, understanding, or correcting idle. They can be among the processes through which what happens is produced. If understanding the new evidence changes a response, the understanding belongs within its explanation. The actual person can learn and change through capacities already present. [S5, §§5.1–5.5; S8, §§10.1–10.3]

The earlier question about universal determinism should nevertheless retain its proper scope. An account of this person's present constitution and the absence of a further pilot does not need to wait for a demonstration that every event in the universe has a uniquely fixed successor. Connection and strict determinism are different claims. The existing examination explicitly preserves that distinction. [S8, §§10.4–10.5]

The return from woodcarver to cello beginner depends on constituted agency. Strict determinism can be the stipulated setting in which the argument is explored; it is not a new conclusion proved by the examples. No premise about quantum physics, predictability, or a completed future is needed.

Agency language and connected language can describe the same event. “I listened, reconsidered, and changed what I did” identifies an actual activity. Describing the capacities and circumstances through which it happened gives another part of the account. A reason for using either description does not require declaring the other a fiction.

The person also remains particular. Connection does not dissolve the beginner into an undifferentiated whole. Her position, capacities, reach, and relationships identify the local context through which this activity occurs.

This preserves the difference between actual participation and independent self-origination. The first is available throughout the examples. The second is not needed to make the first real.

## 9. Flow and the direction of a life

Flow entered the conversation as a possible description of the carver's absorbed engagement. The relevant feature was activity proceeding through developed understanding with little explicit self-supervision. The argument does not depend on a technical diagnosis that she is in a particular psychological state.

The cello return changes what can be concluded from the example. Absorption may be valuable and deeply expressive. It cannot become the admission condition for actual selfhood.

Nor does absorption establish correspondence. A smoothly executed movement can misread the material. A difficult pause can make a correction possible. If the carver encounters an unfamiliar defect, stopping to examine it may be an expression of her understanding. If something outside the task needs attention, continuing to carve without interruption may no longer fit the situation.

There can be reasons to make room for absorbed work: the person's enjoyment, the development of a capacity, and participation in something significant all belong in the account. These are reasons to examine in their actual relationships. They do not become a universal instruction to maximize time in flow.

John asked whether the account suggests an “is/does” connection. The connection is present in the activity. A mind contextualizes, and the relationships it holds can guide its next step. The carver's understanding of this wood participates in what she does with it. The beginner's understanding of the sound and instruction participates in her correction.

Practical questions remain within that same inquiry. Why continue? Why cultivate this capacity? Why interrupt, rest, or attend to someone else? The person, their purpose, the proposed response, and the actual dependencies are all among the things to understand. No further chooser outside the examination has to convert an account into an exempt command. [S3, §14a; S5, §§6.1–6.3]

This supplies a direction through the circumstances. It does not derive a mission to maximize cognition, skill, introspection, or a particular feeling at every moment. Such a mission would need further grounds. Sometimes the relevant understanding is already sufficient; sometimes learning is required; sometimes the actual cost of continuing supplies a reason to stop.

The further possibility developed here is that understanding can become more integrated with how the person lives. That possibility includes explicit reasoning and ordinary activity as well as practiced responsiveness. It need not be measured by whether the experience resembles the carver's.

We cannot simply command ourselves into mastery or decide that thinking will cease. Nor is either required. What can be released is the demand that our activity must display independent conscious authorship before we can recognize it as our own.

## 10. Held in God across the whole range

The theological conclusion follows the same person through all the conditions already considered.

In CT, God names the context with no outside. The self is a local context with an outside: particular, situated, dependent, and able to act through relationships with what is beyond its local edge. Its being nested within the whole does not dissolve that particularity. [S1, §4; S7, §2]

The master is within that context. The beginner is within it. The bodily conditions shaping their activity, the thoughts through which they understand, the capacities unavailable to conscious inspection, and the experiences of choosing are all within it.

There is no stage at which a thought first has to prove that it was independently originated in order for the person thinking it to belong. There is no stage at which deliberation carries the person outside the relations constituting them. And there is no stage at which a loss of explicit self-supervision removes them from those relations.

This is the force of John's statement that we are connected in our unfolding, “all the way down...and back.” The account follows the activity through its constituents, its encounters, its representations, and its reflection upon itself. Each further thing actually found belongs within the same context. No final layer requires an outside position.

### 10.1 The relation and its recognition

Being connected, understanding connection, and feeling connected are distinct.

Being connected concerns the actual relationships through which the person exists and acts. Understanding concerns the account the person can hold of those relationships. Feeling connected concerns how some part of that life is experienced.

The understanding can improve. The feeling can become vivid, quiet, or absent. Those developments are real changes within the person's life. They do not create the underlying fact that the person is in context.

An experience of disconnection is itself an actual experience within connection. It can disclose loneliness, lack of support, unfamiliarity with oneself, or some other real condition that deserves attention. Its reality does not establish an outside to reality.

The person who understands the connected self while feeling awkward at the cello is therefore fully included. So is the person who has no such philosophical understanding. Intellectual acknowledgment is not the condition that first makes the acknowledged relation actual.

### 10.2 Held includes the particular life

“Held” in this account is more substantial than a generic label for membership in a set. What is included is this person's actual life: their capacities and limits, dependencies and attachments, hopes and injuries, mistakes and attempts to repair.

Within CT's account of stakes, those dependencies have their actual extent. They are not reduced because the person cannot feel their significance, enlarged merely by the person's position, or made unreal by somebody else's failure to notice. The person's own stake is included at its size.

This connects the no-outside account with CT's account of held love. The particular life is retained in its actual relations and significance. A loving reader can encounter that life from a position; their reading does not originate the reality it encounters. The relevant earlier papers distinguish what is held, what a reader sees, and what is felt. [S7, §2; S9, §§2–3]

The beginner's unfamiliarity with herself does not remove anything actual from this inclusion. She does not have to become impressive, effortless, or philosophically accomplished before her needs and possibilities belong in the account.

At the same time, full inclusion does not make all states interchangeable. Her skill can develop; an error can remain an error; a burden can be larger or smaller. What happens still matters. The constancy is in the inclusion of the actual person and what is actually at stake, not in an assertion that every outcome is equally good.

### 10.3 Holding recognition feeling and help

The conclusion keeps four relationships distinct:

| Relationship | What it names |
| --- | --- |
| Held | The particular life and its actual dependencies belong fully within context. |
| Recognized | A situated reader encounters or understands some of that reality. |
| Felt | An experience of connection, love, belonging, or significance occurs in a particular life. |
| Helped | An actual response changes conditions or meets a need. |

Being held does not wait for the other three. The other three, when they occur, are additional realities within the holding. The corpus's “seen love” makes a further claim about faithful regard: the actual life or stake is encountered at its size. Ordinary recognition alone does not establish that.

This distinction keeps the assurance exact. The holding asserted here does not supply an extra cosmic observer or perform an act of care that nobody has performed. An unmet need remains unmet until something happens that meets it. The whole context includes both the need and whatever responses occur. [S7, §§2, 9–11]

Nor does completeness of inclusion predict a future outcome or establish indefinite personal continuation. It says that nothing actual in the person's life has to stand outside the context to be what it is.

### 10.4 The recognition is already within the holding

When the person comes to understand herself as held, that recognition occurs within the reality to which she already belongs.

This understanding can matter. It can alter the account through which she encounters difficulty, conscious choice, or the absence of a particular feeling. It can become operative in the next moment of her life.

Yet the understanding itself has no exemption from the structure it recognizes. It arises through this person, in these circumstances, through what has reached her. The recognition is part of the connected unfolding.

That gives the conclusion its particular form of assurance. There is no state of mastery, no level of felt selfhood, and no achievement of philosophical clarity that must first be attained for the person to be held. Recognizing the relation can change how it is inhabited; the person need not successfully produce that change to qualify for the relation.

The woman remains at the cello, consciously uncertain, trying to understand what she is doing. Her belonging does not await the day when the fingering becomes effortless.

## 11. What this recognition can change

The practical opening is a release of an unnecessary requirement: the demand that thoughts and actions must originate in a separate author for them to be the person's own.

This leaves the activities themselves intact. Deliberation remains real. Learning can change a person. Understanding can participate in action. Mistakes and their consequences remain available to correction.

The framing also removes a possible demand created by the earlier mastery example. A person need not try to suppress thought, manufacture absorption, or maintain an uninterrupted feeling of connection. Those efforts could themselves become another way of withholding recognition from ordinary life.

The person can feel deeply herself in a particular activity and value that experience. She can also recognize herself when the experience is absent. Felt depth is something that can matter in her life; it is not the certificate that makes the life hers.

This is a possible change in lived understanding, not a promise that accepting the argument immediately changes how someone feels. The body, relationships, and circumstances remain part of what makes an understanding available and operative. A need for practical support cannot be replaced by insisting that the person adopt the right description. [S7, §§1, 3 and 9–11]

The account nevertheless gives the recognition somewhere familiar to begin. The person may already accept the master's constituted activity as fully hers. The return to the beginner then carries that acceptance into the very experience that seemed to demand a separate chooser.

What changes is the account of who is doing the thinking. There is no need to wait for thinking to stop.

## 12. Contribution and scope

### 12.1 What this conversation adds

The existing corpus already supplies the connected self, real agency within formation, contextualization through skill, the inclusion of feeling, and the distinction between being held and feeling held. This paper brings them together through a specific argumentative return.

The first reversal uses mastery to loosen the identification of self with an explicit conscious director. The second reversal returns to the same person as a deliberate beginner and prevents mastery from acquiring exclusive authority over actual selfhood.

That is the clarification to carry forward. A summary that ends with the carver, praises greater occlusion as the deeper self, or makes flow the goal has stopped before the decisive step.

The conversation also joins the clarification to embodied recursion. What shapes the mind's encounter becomes part of what the mind can encounter; the resulting understanding can then shape the next moment. The self's representation of itself stays within the self it is representing.

The theological conclusion extends through the whole range. The actual person is held in God across variations in explicit thought, occlusion, felt self, and intellectual recognition. Recognition can deepen how the relation is lived without being the condition that creates it.

### 12.2 Which claims need more than this argument

The constitutive conclusion does not require an independent demonstration of strict universal determinism. The conversation uses determination as its setting; CT's existing examination keeps the stronger physical claim distinct.

A claim that occlusion alone explains every human experience of freedom would require a further psychological argument. The present account distinguishes the real experience, its interpretation, and the possible effects of retaining that interpretation.

A claim that flow is the best state for every person or the proper goal of a whole life would require grounds concerning the actual stakes, alternatives, and costs. It does not follow from the carver's mastery.

Being fully constituted and fully included does not make every action equally adequate or every exercise of agency equally unconstrained. Differences in understanding, coercion, control, harm, and benefit remain real. The paper removes a hierarchy of actual selfhood based merely on explicit deliberation; it does not erase those other distinctions.

Finally, the holding asserted here does not add a separately intending whole, guaranteed rescue, or a proof about every future event. Those conclusions would need premises beyond the argument given.

### 12.3 What is whose and how to continue

John supplied the developing questions, the mind/body and occlusion sequence, the woodcarver's initial reversal, the same-person cello return, the continuum of self-experience, and the culminating recognition of being held in God. He asked that the development be written up and placed where new conversations would encounter it.

The assistant supplied the exposition, the separation of explicit supervision from consciousness, the distinctions among bodily condition, felt presentation, and warrant, the worked exhaustion example, the arrangement of the dimensions, and the source connections. The stronger claim that mastery alone reveals the fuller actual self is corrected by John's final return.

The paper records a substantive conceptual clarification within the stated CT framework. It makes no claim to a new formal verification or an exhaustive historical comparison. Particular experiential effects remain possibilities to understand through actual lives.

Future discussions can take up how this recognition becomes operative in ordinary life. They should retain the full return argument rather than reopening a requirement to reach flow, reduce thought, or feel connected before one can be the connected self.
