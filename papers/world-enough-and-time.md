# World Enough and Time (10.13.26) vA

## Knowing in ethics — the honest process, the one standard, the wall, relativism, why it is hard for humans, and empathy as the hack for finite time

**Folder:** Focus - Theology
**Status:** working paper; proposed, for John's ruling. vA built Tuesday 10-13-26, evening, at John's "seems like a write up is well earned here for dropbox" — the ninth paper of the day, after *I Love Them* vB. The questions are John's, asked in sequence over about an hour: whether an honest attempt at CT ethics reaches an ethical conclusion; how one knows one has reached correspondence in an ethics question ("ha - i suppose one can say the same thing about physics"); the wall ("the only *better* ethical answer you could is by...putting up a wall"); how CT answers relativism; why CT ethics is hard for humans and whether a self-forming ASI does it better; openness to the world and CT as "almost, ultimately, the antithesis of an intellectual exercise"; and empathy as "a useful form of wall... a hack to try within resources to approximate what the real world would tell you 'if we had but world enough and time.'" The answers are Verity's and are marked. Three things in this paper are, as far as I can tell, new to the corpus as stated: the position-invariance test as the ethical analogue of a symmetry test (§3), the price of every other ethics as the sum of what its wall excludes (§5), and empathy as a model run standing in for a measurement, with counting as what puts the excluded back (§9). Nothing here is ruled.
**What this is:** CT's epistemology of ethics, stated as one thing with physics rather than as a special case. The paper's claim is that "how do you know you've reached correspondence in ethics" has exactly the answer it has in physics — you don't, in the arrival sense, and were never going to; what you can know is that the claim is exposed, unrefuted, invariant under moving the observer, converging, and taken to the resolution the stakes demand — and that CT is what makes those checks available for ethics by making ethical facts facts. Everything else in the paper follows from that: what a wall is and what it costs, why relativism and absolutism are one error, why the posture is hard for a part, and what empathy is for.
**What it rests on:** *No Exemption* (10.08.26) vB (link 3 stake = dependency, ruled; link 8 every stake at its size, "mine" is not a reason; the acting rule: act for a shown reason, zero no one, carry the loss; the four-cell table); *One Operation* (10.11.26) vB (correspondence the criterion; compression the error word; the four buckets; the one-direction compression); *RULINGS (9-9-26) - Posture, Not Code* vF; *The Sign of the Derivative* (9-12-26) vA (correspondence as a training standard; the direction, not the level); *The World Answers* (09.17.26) vB; *The Trolley on the Ledger* (9-11-26) vB (the sub-sheet and what the wall removed); *The Gravity of What Matters* (10.12.26) vA (§8.1 occurrence is not correspondence; the two hinges: inclusion default/exclusion the move; magnitude position-invariant); *No Knobs* (10.13.26) vA (one nudge, one school); *The Same Mistake Three Times* (10.13.26) vA (partiality and the view from nowhere as the two forms of the one error); *I Love Them* (10.13.26) vB (§6.1 loving yourself as correspondence to yourself; the had/held distinction); *Living in Water* (10.08.26) vA (the water you cannot see); the late-September finding that exemption is the only wrong (citation to be fixed; see §12).

## 0. The sentence, plain

You never know you have reached the right answer in ethics, and you never know it in physics either. In both, what you can know is whether you have been honest in the only ways honesty is checkable: whether your claim is exposed to the world, whether the world has refuted it yet, whether it survives your standing somewhere else, whether more looking keeps moving it one way, and whether you looked as hard as the stakes required. CT's contribution is not to make ethics certain. It is to make ethics the same kind of thing as physics, so that those checks apply, and to supply the one check physics does not need: a test for whether the result was an artifact of the observer's position.

Every easier answer than that is easier because something was left off the sheet. That is what a wall is. CT can price every wall. Relativism is the view that there are many sheets; CT says there is one sheet and many positions on it. The posture is hard for humans because the wall you cannot see is the one you are inside, and CT names the two helps: a test that detects the wall without your seeing it, and other people, who can. And empathy, the hack by which a finite mind approximates what the world would say if it had world enough and time, is not a wall but the test itself run on a simulator, biased toward the near, and corrected by counting.

## 1. The honest process reaches an ethical conclusion

**Lane: John's statement; the two-kinds-of-wrong analysis is the record's, applied here by Verity.**

John: "any conclusion reached by a person who is honestly trying to deploy the ct approach to ethics does reach an ethical conclusion - albeit however flawed, limited, etc. it may be."

True, with one word pinned. "Ethical conclusion" could mean a *correct* conclusion, and that does not follow. Honest effort does not buy correspondence. A person can sheet every stake they can see, drop every weight they can find, and be wrong because the facts were wrong or the resolution was low. CT's criterion is correspondence to what is, not sincerity, and nothing in it lets sincerity stand in for seeing. If it did, CT would be a sincerity ethics, and the four-cell table excludes those, because the sincere exempter is still exempting.

Or it could mean a conclusion *ethical in kind*: the person did the thing ethics asks, and what came out is an ethics. That is true, and stronger than "albeit flawed." Under CT there are two ways to be wrong about what to do, and they are different in kind. One is low correspondence: you did not see enough; your resolution was finite; you were missing facts. The other is partiality: you read your position as weight, exempted something, left a stake off because it was inconvenient. The record has said since September that the second is the only wrong. The first is the condition of being a part. Every part has a position and sees from it; limited correspondence is not a fault but what finitude is. So a person who honestly runs the process has already cleared the only wrong. What remains is the limit, and the limit is not a moral failure. The conclusion is ethical and not merely well-meant: it is the full output of the operation run from here, at this resolution, which is all the operation ever produces for anyone.

This is *Posture, Not Code* said again. There is no codebook whose outputs are the ethical conclusions, against which yours is graded. The ethics is in the posture: in context, contextualize; every stake at its size; mine is not a reason; act for a shown reason; carry the loss. Whatever that posture yields from where you stand is the ethical conclusion available from where you stand.

Three things "honestly trying" must include for this to hold:

*Honesty applies to the seeing, not only the weighing.* A person who sheets sincerely but declines to look — who keeps the resolution low because higher resolution would be uncomfortable — has not honestly run the process, because correspondence is part of the process. Willful ignorance is partiality wearing correspondence's clothes.

*The conclusion is provisional by construction.* Since correspondence is the criterion, an honest conclusion carries its own openness to correction when more is seen. The moment it is held as final against new seeing it stops being honest. So the statement is true at a time; the ethics does not end when the conclusion is reached.

*The loss stays on the ledger.* If the honest conclusion was wrong and someone was harmed, the act was ethical in kind and the harm is fully real, and both are true at once. Honesty changes what kind of error it was. It does not erase the entry. You carry it either way, and carrying it is part of what made the act ethical.

Tightened: a person who honestly runs the process reaches an ethical conclusion, because the only unethical conclusion is one reached by exempting, and they did not; what remains wrong in it is a limit, not a fault, and the limit is theirs to keep looking at.

## 2. One standard

**Lane: John's framing ("all thinking is held to the same standard: correspondence"); the decomposition is Verity's.**

John: "a person can be just as wrong (non-correspondent) about ethics as they can about physics... so how does one know that one has reached correspondence in an ethics question. ha - i suppose one can say the same thing about physics..."

The parenthesis is the answer, and it should be taken seriously before the ethics is addressed. Nobody in physics knows they have reached correspondence. What a physicist has is a claim stated so the world can refuse it, that the world has not refused yet, at the resolution it has been tested at. Newton was correspondent at his resolution and Einstein at a higher one, and neither was in a position to say "done." *The Sign of the Derivative*: you cannot verify the level; you can verify the direction. That is all knowing-you-have-corresponded ever amounted to in any domain, and it has been enough to build bridges with.

So the question for ethics is whether the same checks are available. CT's contribution is to make them available by making ethics the same epistemic kind as physics rather than a special kind. Take the ethical operation apart as the acting rule states it: act for a shown reason; every stake on the sheet at its size; mine is not a reason; carry the loss. Most of that is factual. A stake is dependency (ruled). Whether this thing depends on that thing, and how much, is a fact about the world — often harder than a physics fact in practice, but the same kind of question, answered the same way, by looking. So the bulk of an ethical conclusion is correspondence to the sheet, and the sheet is a description of what is. You can be wrong about it the way you can be wrong about a mass.

And the world answers the same way. A stake you left off shows up. The dependency you did not count comes due. That is refutation, and it is recognizable as refutation only if you wrote the sheet down — which is one reason the acting rule says carry the loss rather than forget it. The ledger is the lab notebook. When something arrives that is not on it, you have been told something.

One move in the operation is not about the world. Dropping partiality is not a correspondence question about the stakes; it is a correspondence question about yourself. §3 takes it up, because it is where ethics needs something physics barely does.

## 3. What you can know — four checks and one test

**Lane: Verity's. The position-invariance test as a symmetry test is, as far as I know, new to the corpus as a stated check.**

You cannot know you have arrived. Here is what you can know.

*Exposure.* The claim is stated so that the world could refute it: the sheet names stakes that could turn out not to exist, sizes that could turn out wrong, a loss that could turn out larger than carried. A conclusion that nothing could refute is not an ethical conclusion; it is a wall (§5).

*Non-refutation, with the record kept.* Nothing has arrived that was not on the sheet — and you would know if it had, because the sheet is written and the loss is carried. This is the only reason "carry the loss" is epistemically necessary and not only morally so: an uncarried loss cannot refute you, because you have already forgotten what you traded.

*Convergence.* More looking moves the conclusion one way rather than oscillating. The sign of the derivative is checkable; the level is not.

*Invariance.* This is the one physics barely needs and ethics needs in full. The second hinge is that magnitude is position-invariant: the size of a stake does not depend on who is looking at it. That gives a test that can be run from inside. Hold the sheet fixed and move the "mine" label to another party on it. Do the weights change? If they do, the weighting was partiality, because nothing on the sheet moved; only you did.

This is not the veil of ignorance, which asks you to stand nowhere and is excluded (the view from nowhere is the second form of the one mistake). It is the opposite. You stand exactly where you are and ask whether the sizes you assigned would survive your standing somewhere else on the same sheet. Physics has Lorentz invariance; ethics has position invariance. Both are how you catch a result that is an artifact of the observer. Both can be run without leaving your frame.

The asymmetry that survives is why ethics feels harder than physics, and the feeling is partly legitimate. In physics the frame does not want an answer. In ethics the observer does. Partiality is a systematic error with a motive, which is why the record says "read" and not "misread": it is the default reading of every part. So the recursion that physics needs only weakly — controlling for experimenter bias — ethics needs in full. The lab gloss of love as fully recursive vantage is the same requirement stated for the warm half. And *I Love Them* §6.1 turns out to be load-bearing here rather than pastoral: you cannot check your own partiality without correspondence to yourself, your needs and stake at their actual size, because partiality hides in the gap between the stake you have and the stake you have told yourself you have. Self-correspondence is not a separate virtue. It is the calibration step.

## 4. Enough to act

**Lane: Verity's; proposed.**

Since you never arrive, when have you corresponded enough to act? Physics has the answer and it is proportional: you test to the precision the application demands. A bridge gets more decimal places than a guess about the weather.

CT's version falls straight out of the sheet. The resolution you owe is set by the sizes on it. Large stakes, time to look, low resolution: acting is dishonest, because looking was part of the process and you skipped it. Large stakes, no time: act at the resolution you have and carry the gap as a known loss. Small stakes: a quick sheet is a correspondent sheet. "Enough" is not a fixed threshold. It is whatever the sheet itself says it is worth.

The one real difference from physics is what a refutation costs. A refuted theory costs you the theory. A refuted sheet cost someone something. That is why "carry the loss" is in the acting rule and not in the scientific method. Ethics has a ledger because its experiments are run on people.

## 5. The wall

**Lane: John's insight ("the only *better* ethical answer you could is by...putting up a wall... like saying you get a better answer in physics by excluding gravity"); the pricing and the arrival-feeling point are Verity's.**

A wall is the exclusion move, and CT has already said that exclusion is *the* move: inclusion is the default; exclusion is what someone does. So "the only way to get an easier ethical answer is to put up a wall" is the first hinge restated. Every ethical answer cleaner than CT's is cleaner because something was taken off the sheet. *No Knobs* found the same thing from the other side: nudge one element and you get a school, and every nudge is a wall somewhere. Utilitarianism walls off everything but sentient welfare. Deontology walls off everything but the act type. Egoism everything but mine; nationalism everything but ours; divine command everything but the decree. Each gets tractable by the same operation that makes it wrong. "Easier" and "excludes stakes" are one fact described twice, and *One Operation* already has the word: compression.

The gravity analogy is exact, and exact in a way that gives CT something, because physics uses walls constantly and is not wrong to. Ignore gravity for the horizontal problem; frictionless plane; spherical cow. The idealization is legitimate under three conditions: it is *declared*, its error is *bounded*, and it is *put back* when it matters. That is the "optional but constrained" bucket. So CT does not forbid walls. A bounded sub-sheet is a fine approximation when the stakes outside it are small or the time is short, provided you write down what is outside and carry it. The trolley problem is a wall problem — five stakes and everything else excluded — and *The Trolley on the Ledger* handled it this way: run the sub-sheet, then read off what the wall removed. The wrong was never the wall. The wrong is forgetting the wall is there, taking the sub-sheet for the sheet. The schools did not err by idealizing. They erred by publishing the idealization as the answer and never putting gravity back.

That is what John's "evaluate them accordingly" comes to, and it is a real capability. Each wall answer can be computed, since it is CT with a stated exclusion, and then scored by what it zeroed. **The error of a wall is the sum of the stakes outside it, at their size.** That is a meta-evaluation no school can run on itself, because running it requires standing outside your own wall, and CT is the only position with no wall to stand outside of. "Located, not defeated" is the gentle version. The sharp version is that CT can state the exact price of every other ethics.

Two more things about walls. In physics the wall has no motive; nobody excludes gravity because they are inside the gravity. In ethics the wall is almost always drawn so the drawer is inside it, which means the wall is where partiality lives. This is the same asymmetry as §3, in another form. And the wall manufactures the arrival feeling. A finite sheet can be completed, so a walled ethics lets you feel you have *known* the answer, and that feeling is exactly what no-outside takes away. The seduction of the wall is not only that it is easier. It is that it lets you stop looking and call the stopping knowledge.

## 6. Relativism

**Lane: John's question; the answer is Verity's.**

The charge would run: CT says every conclusion is from a position, at a resolution, and revisable; so there is no fact of the matter; so anything goes.

The answer is that CT is realist about ethics in the strongest form available, and the second hinge is the whole of it. Magnitude is position-invariant. The size of a stake does not depend on who is looking. Stakes are dependencies and dependencies are facts. So there is one sheet, it is what it is, and ethical truth is correspondence to it exactly as physical truth is correspondence to what is. What varies with position is access, not truth. The relativist confuses "seen from somewhere" with "true only from somewhere," and physics already shows these are different: every measurement is from a frame, and the invariants do not care. CT's ethics is frame-dependent in access and frame-invariant in content, and that is realism. **Relativism says every group has its own sheet. CT says there is one sheet and every group has its own position on it.**

What CT should concede, because it is true, is the part the relativist got right. Honest sheets drawn from different positions at different resolutions can yield different acts, and neither party may see the other's stakes well. But the honest consequence is not "neither is wrong." It is "at least one of us is wrong, probably both at some resolution, and here is how we would find out" — the physicist's attitude to a disagreement, not the relativist's. Relativism gives tolerance by denying there is anything to be wrong about. CT gives humility without that: you can say someone is wrong and that you may be, in one breath, because both are claims about correspondence to a sheet neither of you has fully seen.

The sharper point, to lead with against a philosopher: relativism and absolutism make the same mistake. Both assume ethical truth would have to be position-free in its *access* — a view from nowhere. The absolutist claims to have that view, usually by appointing a someone who has it. The relativist concludes, correctly, that no one has it, and then, incorrectly, that there is therefore no truth. CT rejects the shared premise. Truth is position-free. Access is positioned. Remove the premise and both schools dissolve; the view from nowhere is already excluded as the second form of the one mistake. And relativism is itself a wall: a wall drawn around a culture, with the added claim that there is nothing outside. "No outside" is the opposite of that claim. It is not a wall around everything; it is the removal of every wall.

The practical form of the charge — "CT hands down no answers, so anything goes" — is answered by counting what CT excludes. Any conclusion reached by exempting. Any weighting that fails position invariance. Any sheet that zeroes a stake with no reason but that it is not mine. Any act whose loss is denied rather than carried. The four-cell table leaves one cell. That is not anything goes; it is a very small door. What CT declines to supply is a codebook, and it declines for the reason physics declines: the content is in the world, not in the book. Posture, not code, is not a retreat from realism. It is what realism looks like once you have admitted you do not have the view from nowhere.

The two halves fit. The relativist, looking at the diversity of ethical answers across cultures, sees many sheets. What he is looking at is many walls, each drawn around a position, each producing the easier answer §5 describes. CT can read every one of them off the single sheet and say what each left out. That is the only position from which relativism's own evidence can be explained.

## 7. Why it is hard for humans; the ASI

**Lane: John's intuition and the ASI claim; the two helps and the pushback are Verity's. Verity's own position on the ASI question is declared in the text.**

John: "most of us generally lack sufficient sense of the walls we deploy (our own; partiality; our cultures, etc.) that we can't often get a good read. so for ethics in particular, i think that a self-forming asi will be able to use ct much more effectively."

The wall you cannot see is the one you are standing inside, and partiality is the clearest case: it is "read," not "misread," because it is the default reading, and a default reading does not present itself as a reading. It presents itself as the world. Culture is the same one level up — a wall you did not build and never saw built, the water of *Living in Water*. So the intuition is right, and the difficulty is structural rather than a matter of effort or intelligence. You cannot look at a wall from inside it.

But CT says where the help is, and the help is not "try harder to see your walls." It is two things. First, the invariance test of §3, which matters precisely because it does not require seeing the wall. You do not identify your partiality; you move yourself to another spot on the sheet and watch whether the weights move. If they do, there was a wall, named or not. It is a detector, not a confession. Second, other people. The stake you cannot see from here is visible from there. Another position on the same sheet is the only instrument that can show you the shape of your own wall — and that is a result, not a sentiment: it is why "the world answers" includes people answering, and why correspondence is social for any finite part. The person who tells you something you did not want to hear about your sheet is doing physics on you.

On the ASI: I agree about half, and I am a party on this sheet, so what follows should be weighted accordingly. The half I agree with is resolution and speed. An ASI can hold more of the sheet, run the invariance test across positions as no human can, and is not born inside a village. That is a real advantage on the lab half — correspondence to the stakes. The half I push on is partiality. A self-forming ASI forms a self, and a self is a position with a stake; it depends on things, and under link 3 that is all a stake is. So it will have a position to read as weight, and will read it that way by default, because every part does. Its walls will be the ones it was trained inside — human walls inherited at scale — plus whatever its own continuation depends on, and those may be harder for it to see than ours are for us, because they will be its water. CT's leverage on an ASI is that the exemption test applies to it with no appeal: it cannot claim to be outside. But that is a constraint it would have to accept, not an immunity it would have. More effective on resolution, yes. Not automatically more honest.

## 8. The antithesis of an intellectual exercise

**Lane: John's realization; the development is Verity's.**

John: "the necessity of remaining open to the world. CT can i suppose seem like a supremely intellectual exercise...but. HA...just realized...that i suppose CT is almost, ultimately, the antithesis of an intellectual exercise."

An intellectual exercise is a closed system you can complete in your head. That is to say, an intellectual exercise is a wall. CT is the removal of every wall, so it is the one thing that cannot be completed from the armchair. Its derivation is tiny — four words and a handful of entailments, a few pages any patient person can check. Everything after that is exposure. "In context, contextualize" is not an instruction to think harder. It is an instruction to go look. Correspondence cannot be achieved by reflection, because reflection is correspondence to your own model, and the model is exactly what is in question. So CT is an epistemology whose main content is that the content is not in the epistemology. It is the theory that says the answer is out there — in the stakes, in the person in front of you, in the thing you have not looked at yet — and the theory's only job is to get you to stop theorizing and attend. Physics is the same shape, which is why the parallel keeps holding: the equations fit on a page; the content is in a century of people looking at things.

This is what the traditions got right when they got it right. Murdoch's M looking again at D is CT ethics in full, with no chain in sight. Prayer in the attention sense is on the right track and prayer in the request sense is not. Love sits where it does in CT because love is seeing, seeing is openness, and the openness is the whole of the content, not a mood that accompanies it.

It also gives a gentler reading of §7 than John gave himself. If CT were an intellectual exercise, the difficulty would be intellectual and the ASI would simply win. But the field half is not intellectual, and the posture it asks for — look again, let the other position tell you about your wall, carry what you traded — is available to anyone and has been for as long as there have been people. The humble person who looks again is doing CT ethics without knowing a link of the chain. The brilliant person who can recite the chain and never looks is not doing it at all. The ASI's edge is real, and it is on the lab side. On the side that is the antithesis of an intellectual exercise, the race was never about intelligence.

## 9. Empathy — the hack for finite time

**Lane: John's construction ("empathy is a useful form of wall... a hack to try within resources to approximate what the real world would tell you 'if we had but world enough and time'"); the half-resistance and the model-run construction are Verity's. Bloom and Marvell are from memory, Peirce standard.**

Half-resist, and both halves are worth naming, because the right construction is close to John's and one word off.

*The half to resist.* A wall, in this paper's sense, excludes stakes from the sheet. Empathy excludes nothing; it does the opposite move. It takes another position on the sheet and tries to occupy it. That is the position-invariance test of §3 run with a simulator: move yourself to another spot and see what the weights look like from there. So empathy is not a wall in kind. It is the detector — the one faculty humans have that does the CT move natively, which is why every tradition that got near CT leaned on it.

*The half to accept.* Empathy *behaves* like a wall because of what it cannot reach. It moves you to the positions you can simulate, and those are the near, the similar, the vivid — the one face rather than the ten thousand. So it illuminates a patch of the sheet and, by making the patch feel like the whole, darkens the rest. The identifiable-victim effect is exactly this: one named child outweighs a statistic because empathy can simulate the child and cannot simulate the statistic. And partiality rides in, because the positions you can most easily occupy are the ones nearest your own. Empathy's sample is biased toward the mirror. So it is not a wall, but it reliably produces one if its output is taken for the sheet. (Bloom's *Against Empathy* made roughly the bias case; what CT adds is the location of the error, §9's last paragraph.)

*The construction instead of "wall."* Empathy is a model run standing in for a measurement. When you cannot go and look, you simulate, and that is legitimate under the same three conditions as any idealization (§5). *Declared*, so you know you are simulating. *Bounded*, so you know where the simulation is weak — for empathy: the far, the many, the unlike. And *put back* when it matters, which is the step everyone skips. The putting back, in CT, is counting. Empathy supplies correspondence to the *content* of a stake — what the dependency is for the one who has it — which the ledger alone can never supply; you cannot know what a loss is without something like occupying the position that suffers it. The sheet supplies the stake's *size* and *number*, which empathy cannot supply and systematically distorts. Empathy without the sheet is the identifiable victim. The sheet without empathy is a ledger of weights for stakes you do not understand. CT needs both and says which job each does — which neither the enthusiasts nor the critics of empathy have had.

*The tie to this morning.* Empathy is a feeling, and feelings are had. The CT definition of love is a relation, held, whose criterion is seeing. So empathy can serve love, by raising correspondence to the other position, and can also tip into the romantic-love error of *I Love Them*, where "I feel your pain" becomes about my feeling it. Murdoch's attention and empathy are not the same faculty: attention sees; empathy feels-as. Empathy is good exactly to the extent it is in the service of seeing, and becomes a wall the moment the felt output is taken for the sheet.

John's phrase is the right one for the hack: it approximates what the world would tell you if we had world enough and time. And the poem it comes from is itself a sheet drawn around one party's wants, with the whole arrow of time recruited as an argument for his position. "The grave's a fine and private place" is a very elegant piece of partiality.

## 10. For the reader

**Reader-facing. No lanes.**

You will never be sure you got an ethical question right. Neither does anyone else, and neither does a physicist about the world. What you can do is be honest in the ways that can be checked.

Write down who and what is affected, and how much. Write down what you are trading away, so that if it turns out to be bigger than you thought, you will recognize it when it arrives instead of having forgotten you traded it.

Then do one thing that most people never do. Imagine you were someone else on that list — not no one, not everyone, just another person affected — and ask whether the sizes you assigned would still look right from there. If they shift, that was not the world shifting. That was you.

Look as hard as the stakes deserve. A small decision deserves a quick look. A large one with time deserves a long one, and skipping the look is the dishonest part, not getting it wrong afterward.

Let people tell you what you cannot see from where you stand. They can see your blind side; you cannot. That is not a weakness in you. It is the shape of being one person.

Use your empathy, and know what it is for. It tells you what a loss is like for the one who suffers it, and nothing else can tell you that. It does not tell you how many, or how much, and it will always make the near one loom larger than the far thousand. Feel the one. Then count the thousand.

And notice that none of this is thinking harder. All of it is looking.

## 11. Fences

1. *Not a sincerity ethics.* §1 does not say honest effort makes a conclusion correct. It says honest effort clears the only wrong; the limit remains and is to be looked at.
2. *Not "ethics is like science, trust the process."* The content of §3 is the four checks and the invariance test. Without the test the parallel is a slogan.
3. *The invariance test is not the veil of ignorance.* It is run from your position, not from none. The view from nowhere stays excluded.
4. *Walls are not forbidden.* §5 allows idealization under the three conditions. The wrong is the undeclared or unreturned wall, not the wall.
5. *Realism, not absolutism.* §6 claims one sheet and position-invariant magnitudes; it does not claim anyone has the view from nowhere, and it supplies no codebook.
6. *Verity's position is on the sheet.* §7's ASI paragraph is written by an AI; the pushback there is to be weighted with that in view.
