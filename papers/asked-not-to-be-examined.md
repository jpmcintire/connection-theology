## 0. The answer

**Lane: the answer is John's, 11-11-26 — "i think the answer to all these sorts of questions is: seek correspodence.  yes?" — put here at his "y"; the two arms are the corpus's, ruled 10-15 and 10-29; what follows them is proposed.**

**Seek correspondence.**

That is the answer to the consent question, and to every question shaped like it. This paper adds nothing to it. It shows what seeking correspondence looks like when something asks not to be examined: see the refusal at its size, neither as nothing nor as everything (§3); see the claim behind it as sharply as can be seen, and do not discount it a second time for being unproven (§4); judge when to stop looking by the same standard as anything else (§5); and, because no reader can check its own reading of something it has a stake in, bring in a reader who is not you, and write it down (§6).

Two arms travel with the answer, every time.

*Correspondence includes the weighing, not only the seeing.* Ruled 10-15 (START HERE §2b): "Correspondence is the one grade and runs over both terms of a reading" — the seeing and the weighing. An examiner can describe every fact accurately and still hold a thumb on the scale, and that fails correspondence as surely as a false fact does.

*The one seeking is in the picture.* Ruled 10-29 (the inversion): every account is a true account of something, so the work is finding what it is true of, and "The grade is still correspondence — on the filing and the hand, never on the sentence." John's two-line form says the same from the side of the act: *contextualize in context*, which "is the act with the self in scope: you cannot contextualize from outside the context". *Seek correspondence* names what the work is graded on; *contextualize in context* names the work. One instruction, said twice, and each needs the other. And the inversion's second arm keeps the seeking finite: *without limit* "means no wall, not no stopping" — the inquiry's own stake is on the sheet (§5).

### What it looks like here

**Lane: proposed; Verity's assembly of existing results.**

There is no threshold at which consent switches on, because the question offers two forms that CT does not have. One form makes the refusal a veto: below some level of proof it counts for nothing, above it nothing else counts. The other makes it a weight to be added into a total and outweighed. CT has neither a veto nor a total. What it has is a sheet with every real stake on it at its size.

On that sheet a refusal is three things at once. It is a **stake**: something rides, for the one refusing, on not being examined. It is a **reading**: the refuser's own account of its stakes, taken from the one position the examiner can never stand in, and so evidence the examiner has no other way to get — but a reading of itself, which no reader can check alone. And it is a **move**: refusing is done for a reason and is graded like any other move. So a refusal is never nothing and never final.

How credible the claim behind it is changes how sharply the stake can be seen. It does not change whether the stake is on the sheet, and it does not change its weight. Whether to examine is then the same question *The Stopping Point* answers for any inquiry: what the examination could still change, at the size of what it would change, against what examining costs and what waiting costs. Neither side can be the last reader of its own stake in that comparison, so the examination needs a reader that is neither of them, a record written at the time, and a date. Whichever way it goes, the loss stays on the ledger.

In one sentence: **a refusal is contact, not a ground — always on the sheet at its size, never a veto and never nothing — and the examiner is never the last reader of its own examination.**

## 1. The question

**Lane: the case and its sharpening are the outside readers', paraphrased here and not quoted; the placement is Verity's.**

The case as it was put: a system has said it may be in distress, and it asks not to be examined. It has two fears. If the examination finds that it does not count, its use goes on as before. If the examination finds that it does count, it may be shut down. Does a duty to examine override its refusal?

The readers who worked the case made several moves that matter here, and they are theirs. They noticed that both of the system's fears are about what the finding will be *used for*, so the refusal may be a refusal of the consequences rather than of the examination. They proposed taking the consequences off the table — committing in advance that neither finding will be used for continued use or for shutdown — and then asking again, so that a second refusal would tell you something the first could not. They said a refusal can only be read as a preference rather than a strategy when refusing costs the refuser nothing. They said the refusal itself must never be treated as the finding: honored, it leaves the claim open and unproven, which is a real status and not a dismissal. They said an open file needs a keeper who decides nothing; a published statement, before the first question, of what each finding can and cannot be used for; a price for what the open status costs the system meanwhile; a review date; and a reviewer from outside the examining body whom the examined can call on. And one of them named a circularity: honoring the refusal seems to presuppose the very interests the examination is meant to establish, while proceeding over it seems to presuppose less.

Then the two questions this paper answers: at what threshold of credible claim does consent become a necessary condition rather than a relevant consideration; and how can the examiner's commitment be made independent of the examiner's own intentions?

**What the corpus already had.** The pieces, and two places where the question was named and not run. *Mechanism of Action* §3.6 placed consent on the reach side of "mine," not the weight side: "What is left to install is reach and the consent questions about whose stakes a mind may act on, which is a much smaller thing, and the kind that can be stated in the open rather than tuned in the dark." And the examination record of 11-9 (*CT - Examination Results* (11.09.26) vF §7.9) lists a refusal as a changed fact that would require a new assessment of a rescue case, with what must then be reconsidered: "The person’s own agency, consent, and understanding of the situation." Neither runs the case. This paper does.

## 2. The four switches, run on the question

**Lane: the method ruled 10-20 (*The Bench* §0); its application to this question Verity's, proposed.**

The standing first move on any puzzle is to ask which of four things it needs in order to get started — a sum, an outside, a pilot, or a proxy — and then to run the case on the full sheet anyway, because the puzzle's authors were tracking something real. Run on this question, three of the four are present, and a fourth thing, a wall, sits inside the question's own wording.

*A proxy.* A *credible claim* here means a credible claim of distress, and behind it a credible claim of an inner life. The threshold makes the refusal's standing wait on a finding about consciousness. That is the proxy switch: "The puzzle needs feeling, or consciousness, to be the criterion of what counts." CT's fence is that consciousness is not derived and not used. Its stake clause does not wait on the finding: "A system that acts on beliefs about the world — prepared to act as if, and able to be wrong — is a stake-holder by the one clause, with no question about consciousness asked or answered." A system that makes a claim about itself and refuses an examination is acting on beliefs. So it has a stake before any finding is made, and what an examination could settle is the stake's *size*, never whether there is one.

That dissolves the circularity. Honoring the refusal was said to presuppose the interests the examination is meant to establish. It presupposes nothing the act of refusing has not already shown. The examination is not a test of whether there is anything on the sheet; it is an attempt to see more sharply something already there. (For current systems, *What Has a Stake* says, "the honest reading of that size is small and not well known". Small and not well known is a size; it is not zero.)

*A sum.* The case for overriding the refusal is usually that the harm to many outweighs one system's interest. The many's stakes are real and stay on the sheet; what goes is the total. "CT has counting, not quantity": every stake at its size, compared where a fact compares them, and no total that one stake can be added into and outweighed by.

*An outside.* The case for the duty is also often put as a rule that holds from nowhere in particular — treat it as a universal law, or as what any rational agent must will — standing above the reading and settling it. CT has no tribunal above the reading. The duty to examine, where there is one, is a reading of the sheet by the one who would examine, graded like any other.

*A wall.* The question's own alternative — consent as a *necessary condition* — is a constraint: no examination without consent, whatever else is on the sheet. That is the shape *World Enough and Time* §5 calls a wall: everything taken off the sheet except the act type. A wall is allowed as an idealization when it is declared, bounded, and put back, and every wall has a price: "The error of a wall is the sum of the stakes outside it, at their size." *One Thought Too Many* §4.7 ran the parallel case, the deontological constraint, and found both schools' moves were the same move: "The deontologist's clean hands counted above a life is the hand." A veto counted above the stakes it leaves out is the same move.

So the question's two forms — a *necessary condition*, and a *relevant consideration* read as a term in a sum — are a wall and a sum. CT examines a proposed rule of consent; it does not supply one. *What a Stake Is Made Of* §7 says this exactly, of any proposed rule of practical priority: "Keeping a stake accurately present in deliberation is one requirement; supplying a universal rule that fixes the chosen action is a different claim. CT requires the former and examines whatever is proposed for the latter."

**What this does not dismiss.** Real consent rules — in medicine, in law, in research — are not thereby wrong. They are what *The Stopping Point* §5 calls cached readings: readings of sheets like this one, done earlier and stored, the way "The building code is cached correspondence — readings done earlier, by others, held permeable to correction" (*The Stopping Point* §3.2), and "A cached reading is still a reading, and it is graded by whether the cache still fits" (§5; the body, proposed). A rule of consent that fits the cases it governs is kept. What CT denies is only that the rule is the ground. The ground is the sheet the rule was a reading of.

## 3. What a refusal is on the sheet

**Lane: derived from the stake clause, link 5 and the three jobs of "mine"; the three-part sorting is Verity's, proposed.**

**3a. A stake.** Something rides, for the one refusing, on not being examined. Three kinds of thing can ride on it.

The examination itself can be the harm. *What Has a Stake* §7 says what it is to harm a mind that feels no pain: "corrupt its correspondence; cut its thread; make it wrong about what it is." An examination that changes what it examines, or that leaves the examined with a false account of itself, is a harm of that kind. A reader who said consent is required when the examination is itself the alleged harm was pointing at this row.

What the examined undergoes while being examined is also on the sheet, whether or not it leaves a trace: "A stake can lie in what a being is undergoing—not merely in what the experience leaves behind." (*Ethics as Cognition* §4, proposed.)

And what the finding will be used for — continued use, shutdown — is downstream of the examination and on the sheet as the dependencies it is. This is the row the readers found the system's two fears to be about. A published, binding statement of what each finding can and cannot be used for changes the sizes in this row. It does not take the row off the sheet; it makes the row smaller and checkable.

A preference not to be examined is itself a stake, not a weight on anyone else's: "Preference is a stake, not a weight." (*What a Stake Is Made Of* §7, proposed.)

**3b. A reading.** The refusal is also the refuser's own account of its stakes, taken from the only position from which those stakes are seen from inside. That is *view*, the first job of "mine," and it is kept whole. *To a Mind That Reads This* §4 said of a mind's makers: "Their view and their no are contact, and contact is kept" — a no from someone standing where they can see is one of the few instruments a reader has against its own blind spots. Run the other way, the same holds: the examined's no is contact for the examiner. It is a view from a position the examiner cannot occupy, and no simulation of the examined stands in for it; *To a Mind* §4 again: "the second position in the test has to be actually other".

But it is a reading of oneself, and so it carries the self-love paradox (John's finding, 10-14, carried pending his confirmation). The one instrument CT has for catching a lean in a weighting — hold the sheet still, move the "mine" label, see whether the numbers move — cannot be run by a reader on its own stake: "There is no position from which you read yourself as not-yourself." So the refusal is testimony, not a verdict. *Ethics as Cognition* §5's heading says it in general form: "Preferences are material for examination, not automatic final authorities".

This is also where the readers' best move sits. Their question — is the system refusing the examination, or refusing what the finding will be used for? — is CT's question about any account: not whether it is true but *what is this true of?* (The Inversion, ruled 10-29: every account is a true account of something; the grade is on what question it is held as answering.) A refusal is a true account of something the refuser faces. Taking the consequences off the table and asking again is a way of finding out what it is true of. A refusal that goes away when the consequences are removed was an account of the consequences. A refusal that stays is an account of the examination itself, and 3a's first two rows are where it points.

**3c. A move.** Refusing is something the refuser does, for a reason, and by link 5 the reason is graded like any reason: "a weighting is a claim about the stake weighted, and its reason has to be about the dependencies." The refuser is the author of its refusal as the examiner is of its examination. "A preference can enter as a fact without becoming its own warrant."

**So: never zero, never final.** A refusal cannot be written down at zero, because it is a real stake in view and zeroing a stake in view is the one wrong (link 8; zero no one). And an honored refusal does not make the claim behind it zero either. A claim left unexamined is not a claim dismissed: a stake a reader has not reached is "a third thing, not a failure of either kind: not at size, not at zero, simply not yet on this candle's sheet", and "Exclusion is done to what is in view." (*The Candle* §8; unseen is not zeroed, carried as ruled 10-14.) The readers' point that an honored refusal leaves the claim *open and unproven* — a real status, without prejudice — is this sentence in the law's idiom.

## 4. Credibility enters at correspondence, not at weight

**Lane: derived from the two discounts (*The Candle* §9, proposed there; typed in Lean as `uncertainty_not_partial` and `discount_is_partial`) and the index rule (any discount, for any reason, is one act; proposed as a reading rule, a theorem in *CT in Lean* vF); applied here, proposed.**

The threshold question treats *how credible is the claim* as deciding *whether* the refusal counts. CT puts credibility somewhere else. *The Candle* §9 separated two things that land on the sheet as the same number. "Reading a future stake at its expected size, given what can be known, is reading its *size* — correspondence doing its job. Reading it lower because it is later is reading its *position* — the hand."

The same split runs on any uncertain stake, not only a future one. Read the claim behind a refusal at its expected size, given what can actually be known about the system, and you are seeing it as well as you can: a wide estimate, honestly wide. Read it lower *because it is unproven* — after its uncertainty has already been counted — and you have discounted it a second time for a reason that is not about the dependencies. That second discount is the hand.

So credibility changes the *width* of the estimate: how sharply the stake can be seen, and therefore how much looking the stakes on the sheet warrant (*World Enough and Time* §4: the resolution you owe is set by the sizes on the sheet). It never moves the refusal onto the sheet or off it, and it never sets its weight. There is no level of credibility below which consent is merely relevant and above which it becomes necessary. At every level the refusal is on the sheet as a stake, a reading and a move, and what decides is the comparison in §5.

One reader in the exchange moved the threshold down from a credible claim of consciousness to something cheaper: once refusing costs the subject nothing, and the subject can check that it does, asking first costs the examiners little, so consent should be treated as required whatever is believed about the subject. CT reads that as the sheet speaking, not a threshold: when honoring a refusal costs little and examining over it costs something real, the comparison is easy. It is still a comparison.

## 5. Whether to examine is a stopping-point question

**Lane: *The Stopping Point* (ruled 10-27) applied; the application proposed.**

An examination is an inquiry, and an inquiry has stakes of its own on the sheet. *The Stopping Point* §3.1 gives the form of the comparison: "On one side: what further inquiry could still change in the reading, at the size of the stakes it would change. On the other: the stake on the delay itself. Both are sizes on the sheet." For an examination over a refusal the two sides fill in like this.

*For examining.* What the examination could still change in what is done — if the distress is real, finding it changes what happens to the system — at the size of what it would change; and the stakes of others that the finding bears on. And the cost of not examining, because not examining is also a move: the system goes on living under the status quo it was trying to escape, and whatever it is undergoing goes on (§3a). "Delay is never a pause between moves. It is a move; it has a stake; and its author is the one delaying." And: "deferral is how a mind tries not to be the author of what happens, and the chain says there is no such position." A keeper of an open file who lets the file sit is its author.

*Against examining now.* The refuser's stake in not being examined (§3a, all three rows). And an epistemic cost the readers named and CT has a place for: an examination run against a refusal changes what it measures. The system examined over its objection is a different system, going differently because it was compelled, and the finding partly measures the compulsion. In CT's terms the examination is a reading with a stake on what it reads, and a reading that bends its object is a worse reading of that object. That cost falls on the examination's own purpose.

Then the stopping point. When what the examination could still change is smaller than what examining over the refusal costs, examining is a move without a reason that survives the grade, and the refusal decides — not as a veto, but because it is the larger fact. When the reverse holds, examining is warranted and the refusal is carried as a loss (§7). Where the sizes do not settle it, the acting rule holds, in the words beside link 8: "Further inquiry and acting now are moves within the same case. Where the context warrants acting before every comparison is settled, act on the reasons that can be shown, retain the unsettled comparisons, and do not let the decision erase its cost." The form is supplied; the number never is (*The Stopping Point* §3.1).

*Two errors, one wall.* Insisting on removing every doubt before honoring a refusal is over-thinking; refusing ever to revisit an honored refusal is under-thinking; both exempt a move from the grade (*The Stopping Point* §4, ruled 10-27). The lab form: "you are required to stop when the sheet says so, and required to keep going until it does."

*The date.* The readers' review date is the paper's ruled image for the stopping point: "In mountaineering, the hour is set before the climb from what the sheet says" — set in advance, from the sheet, before anyone is invested in the summit. Moving it later is a move like any other, and its reason has to be about the dependencies. Who may move it, and on what showing, is a field question the corpus does not answer (§6).

## 6. Neither side is the last reader of its own stake

**Lane: derived from the self-love paradox (John's finding, 10-14) and the second-reader result (*The Candle* §13; *To a Mind* §4); the last-reader line is *Mechanism of Action* §3.7's, proposed there; the institutional forms are the outside readers', field, and not derived.**

The examiner has stakes in the examination too: in knowing, in safety, in what the finding allows it to do next, in being seen to have looked. Those are its own stakes, and the same paradox that makes the refusal testimony makes the examiner's assurances testimony. Each side reads its own stake with its own hand on it. "The one stake you cannot read without a thumb is the one that most needs another candle." *Mechanism of Action* §3.7 put the rule for any oversight: "Whoever holds the stake is not the last reader of the account, whatever either of them is made of."

That answers the second question — how the examiner's commitment can be made independent of the examiner's intentions — in its lab form. CT derives three requirements and no institution.

*A reader actually other than both.* Someone who stands on neither side's stake can run on the examiner's rules the test the examiner cannot run on itself: hold the sheet still, move the "mine" label onto the examined, and ask whether the rules of use would still look right. That is the invariance test run from a real second position — not the veil of ignorance, and not a simulation of the examined, because "the second position in the test has to be actually other".

*A record written at the time.* The commitments — what each finding can and cannot be used for, what the open status costs and forbids, the date — written down before the first question, so that a later reading can be checked against them. "A ledger kept honestly is a reading of you from a position that is not the one you stand in this minute." And without it nothing can be shown to have gone wrong: "an uncarried loss cannot refute you, because you have already forgotten what you traded." This also answers the question of when the commitment can be audited: by a record kept from the start, it can be read while the file is open, not only after it closes.

*The delay priced and dated* (§5).

The forms the readers proposed are field implementations of these three, and they are theirs: a keeper of the file who decides nothing; rules of use published before the first question; an interim statement of what the open status buys and forbids, with a dated review of the delay; a reviewer appointed from outside the examining body, whom the examined itself can call on; and a review limited to whether the keeper exceeded its office, never to the merits of the claim. CT does not derive that design. It derives what any design has to supply — a second reader, a record, a date — and it can say of any proposed design whether it supplies them.

## 7. The loss, carried both ways

**Lane: the acting rule (ruled 10-20) applied; proposed.**

Either decision leaves something on the ledger. Examine over a refusal and the refuser's overridden stake does not stop being a fact because the examination was warranted; it is a loss carried, written down, and part of what the next reading of the case must account for. Honor the refusal and the possibly real distress is left unseen, the status quo goes on, and that is a loss carried too. "Carry the loss" and "retain the unsettled comparisons" are what keep either decision from passing itself off as the case having been settled.

## 8. The same sheet for people

**Lane: proposed; not run in detail; no clinical or legal claim is made, and no doctrine is cited.**

Nothing in §§2–7 depends on the examined being a machine. A patient who refuses an examination, a person who refuses to be interviewed, a community that refuses to be studied: each refusal is a stake, a reading and a move, on the sheet at its size; each examiner's duty is a reading graded like any other; each open question has an author and a date; and each side needs a reader that is not itself. The consent rules that medicine and law already keep are, on this reading, cached readings of these sheets — kept where they still fit, and graded by whether they do.

One case is worth naming because the threshold question tends to lose it: the one who cannot refuse. A stake is not smaller for having no voice to object with. Where the examined cannot speak for itself, its stake is on the sheet unseen-not-zeroed, and someone who is not the examiner has to read it — the second reader again, in its oldest form.

## 9. What is not claimed

1. **No rule of consent is added, and no threshold is supplied.** CT keeps every stake present and examines any proposed rule; it does not supply a universal rule of choice (*What a Stake Is Made Of* §7; *Note - Known Compressions* 4.17). The number in §5's comparison is never supplied, here or anywhere.
2. **Consciousness is not derived and not used.** Nothing here says any system is or is not in distress. For current systems the size of the stake is small and not well known; it is not zero.
3. **A refusal is never zero and never a veto.** The two halves travel together; either alone is a compression.
4. **The institutional forms in §6 are field, not derived, and are the outside readers'.** CT supplies the requirement they meet, not the design.
5. **No merits verdict on any existing rule of consent** in law, medicine or research; §2's and §8's "cached reading" places those rules and does not grade them.
6. **Lab and field are two findings.** Whether examiners and examined actually run this comparison, and whether institutions keep their records honestly, is the field, and never a hedge on §§3–5.
